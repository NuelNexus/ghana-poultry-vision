PART 1. WHAT THE RESEARCH SAYS (AND WHERE IT CAME FROM)
======================================================================

Sources I studied:
- Presec Legon's winning presentation at the Energy Commission SHS
  Renewable Energy Challenge finale (YouTube, 1:15:47 to 1:25:34).
- TED: "TED's secret to great public speaking", Chris Anderson.
- Y Combinator: "How to Pitch Your Startup", Kevin Hale, and
  "The secret to better product demos", Pete Koomen.
- Dominic Colenso: "How to start a speech like a PRO" and
  "How to start a pitch or presentation".
- Engineer Man: "Explaining technical information to non-technical people".
- Judges' write-ups on pitch competitions and hackathon demos, university
  speaking guides, DECA competition guides, and team-presentation guides.

Reddit and Facebook: my search tools are blocked from both sites, so I
could not read their posts directly. Where advice "from Reddit" appears
below, it came second-hand through blogs that summarised those threads.
Everything else came straight from the sources above.

The 10 rules that kept coming up:

1. Your first 30 seconds have one job: make them listen.
   No "good morning, my name is, our project is titled". Walk out, stop,
   breathe, look at the judges, then hit them with a surprise: a number,
   a question, a short story, or a joke if you're good at jokes.
   (Colenso)

2. One idea, all the way through. Everything links back to it.
   (TED) Your through-line: "We built the software for the farm we
   actually have, not the one in the textbook."

3. Build from what the audience already knows. Use their words and a
   picture they already have in their heads. "108 kilobytes" means
   nothing; "the size of one WhatsApp photo" lands instantly.
   (TED, Engineer Man)

4. Say WHAT it is, with real nouns, before HOW it works. No buzzwords:
   "AI-powered IoT smart platform" makes judges stop listening. Tell it
   so your mum would get it. (Y Combinator, Kevin Hale)

5. A demo is a story about a user, not a tour of pages.
   "This is the login page, this is the dashboard..." is boring.
   Become the farmer and solve her problem. (Y Combinator, Pete Koomen.
   Presec did exactly this: the presenter became the patient.)

6. A simple demo that works beats a clever demo that crashes. Keep a
   backup video. (hackathon judges; Presec's internet failed mid-demo)

7. Numbers must be real and defensible. Judges remember a pitch that
   overclaims. (pitch-competition judges; your scoring sheet also gives
   marks for honesty in Q&A)

8. Pause at every change of section. Nervous speakers rush. Pick three
   spots in the room (left, middle, right) and finish a whole sentence
   on one before moving. (university speaking guides, DECA)

9. Hand-offs: one-line recap of your part, then name the next person.
   Rehearse every hand-off with a stopwatch. (team-presentation guides)

10. Respect the clock exactly. Going over costs marks on your sheet
    (more than 2 minutes over = minus 1 mark).

PART 3. THE SCRIPT
======================================================================

Lines in [brackets] are stage directions. Don't say them.
*Word* = lean on that word.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
OPENING  (about 0:00 - 0:40)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[Take over from the previous speaker. Walk to the centre. STOP.
Breathe. Look at the judges for two full seconds before you speak.]

Most farm apps are built on three assumptions.

One: the internet is *always* on.
Two: the sensors *never* break.
Three: every farmer reads *English*.

[Beat. Slight smile.]

In Ghana, we have a name for those assumptions.

We call them... fiction.

[Let the room laugh. Don't rush it.]

So we asked one question. What if the software was built for the farm
we *actually* have?

[Pause.]

I'm [your name], and I built PoultryGrid's software. It has three
layers: the *Brain*, the *Dashboard*, and the
*Voice*.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
LAYER ONE: THE BRAIN  (about 0:40 - 1:50)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[Hold up the Raspberry Pi.]

Layer one: the Brain.

This small computer lives inside the poultry house. Every thirty
seconds, it reads the sensors and checks every reading: is it missing,
impossible, or stuck on one number?

Then our own neural network weighs twenty-six measurements, including
how far it can *trust* each sensor.

The whole model is one hundred and eight kilobytes. That's about the
size of *one WhatsApp photo*. And it runs with no internet at all.

[Pause. Step forward.]

And this is our key innovation.

Our AI knows when its own sensors are *lying*.

We trained it on six thousand simulated poultry houses, where we broke
the sensors *on purpose*. When the temperature sensor sends rubbish,
our earlier model was right only forty-nine percent of the time.
That's a coin toss.

The new one? Eighty-four percent.

And false "critical" alarms fell from seventy-four percent... to six.

Because a smoke alarm that rings every day is a smoke alarm everybody
ignores.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
LAYER TWO: THE DASHBOARD  (about 1:50 - 2:40)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

[Turn to the screen. Point.]

Layer two: the Dashboard.

Every reading flows from the Pi to our web app. The farmer sees every
house, live.

Now, some of you are thinking: "This is Ghana. What happens when the
network goes?"

[Pull out the network cable slowly so everyone sees it.]

There goes the internet.

[Warm the sensor with your hand. Wait for the fan.]

And the fan *still* switches on. The readings wait on the Pi and
arrive, in order, when the network returns. Nothing is lost.

[Plug the cable back in.]

Because of time, I'll leave the rest of the pages for your questions.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
LAYER THREE: THE VOICE  (about 2:40 - 3:30)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Layer three: the Voice. And this is the part I'm *proudest* of.

Let me be the farmer for ten seconds.

[Tap the microphone on Ama's page.]

"Ama, me nkokɔ no ho te sɛn?"

[Let Ama answer out loud. Don't talk over her. Then turn to the judges.]

She answered me in Twi, and reminded me that today is day eight:
the first Gumboro vaccine, in the drinking water, no chlorine.

The whole site speaks seven languages, and every button can read
itself out loud.

So a farmer who has never read a single word of English can still run
the *entire* system.

[Slow down. Look left, middle, right, one line each.]

So: a brain that works when the internet doesn't.
An AI that knows when its own sensors are lying.
And a voice that speaks the farmer's language.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
ENDING A: SPEAKER 1 DOES THE OUTRO  (about 3:30 - 3:40)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

That's how PoultryGrid *thinks*.

[Turn your body toward Speaker 1 as you say the name.]

[Speaker 1's name] will close for us.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
ENDING B: YOU DO THE OUTRO  (about 3:30 - 4:00)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Remember those three assumptions? We didn't build for that fiction.

We built for the farm at two in the morning: dark, offline, and
speaking Twi.

[Pause.]

And at two in the morning... PoultryGrid is still awake.

We are [School]. This is PoultryGrid. Thank you very much.

[Stand still for two seconds. Then the whole team steps forward
for questions.]

On stage:
- Walk out, stop, breathe, then speak. Silence first looks confident.
- Pause at every "Layer one / two / three". Those are your breaths.
- Finish one sentence on one face. Left, middle, right.
- No notes in hand. Know the three layer names and the closing list
  by heart; the rest flows from them.
- When the internet is pulled, move slowly. The pause IS the drama.
- If something breaks for real: smile, say what it should do, move on.
  Presec's internet died and they still won.

PART 6. QUESTIONS YOU SHOULD EXPECT (SHORT ANSWERS)
======================================================================

Answer first, point at something, stop. Under 45 seconds.

Q: Did you build the AI, or is it ChatGPT?
A: Two AIs. The farm brain is ours: we built the simulator, trained the
   model, and it runs on the Pi with no internet. Ama uses OpenAI's GPT
   models with GhanaNLP's Khaya for Ghanaian languages. We wrote her
   instructions and connected her to the live farm data and the vaccine
   schedule.

Q: Was it trained on real farm data?
A: Honestly, mostly simulated: 6,000 simulated poultry houses, about
   462,000 readings, built around our exact sensors. We're now
   collecting real readings to retrain it.

Q: What happens with no internet?
A: The brain and the fan keep working, and readings are saved on the Pi
   and sent in order later. The dashboard and Ama need internet; the
   part that keeps the birds alive doesn't.

Q: What if a sensor breaks?
A: Every reading is checked first. If the temperature sensor dies, the
   Pi uses its last good reading and keeps track of how old it is, and
   the app shows a "sensor problem" warning.

Q: Why not just a thermostat?
A: A thermostat has one number. Our target moves with the flock's age,
   from 33 °C on day one down to about 20 °C, and it also weighs gas
   and humidity. Cold chicks need the fan OFF; a thermostat can't know
   that.

Q: Is the farm's data safe?
A: Each account sees only its own farm. Passwords are stored hashed, not
   as plain text. Each Pi has its own secret key, and the site runs over
   HTTPS.

Q: How could government roll this out?
A: Through structures that already exist: kits assembled by
   TVET-trained technicians, farmers joining through cooperatives with
   instalments, and Ghana Enterprises Agency advisors and extension
   officers registering farms. We'd start with one district, 20 farms,
   six months, and measure bird deaths, power costs and hatch rates.

If you don't know:
"Good question. We haven't tested that yet. What we have measured is
[closest fact]."
